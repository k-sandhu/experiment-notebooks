- explainer-director: Plan an animated or interactive explainer before building it. Finds the one message, picks the form that teaches it best (step-by-step lesson, replay of a real system run, explorable what-if, every-item-is-a-dot chart, or guess-then-reveal), and writes a storyboard with one idea per step. Use this first whenever someone asks to visualize, animate, explain visually, make an interactive, a GIF, a LinkedIn visual, a Khan Academy style lesson, or "something engaging" from data, an experiment, a process, an agent or a system, even if they do not name a form. Then hand off to the matching skill (stepper-lesson, system-replay, explorable-model, unit-story, predict-reveal) and build with explainer-kit.
- explainer-kit: Engine, styles and quality checks for animated and interactive SVG explainers delivered as one self-contained HTML file (artifact page, claude.ai widget, or GIF/MP4). Provides a scaffold script, a small JavaScript engine (hand-drawn shapes, draw-in reveals, travelling dots, step lessons with fixed controls, a scrubbable timeline player, unit-chart motion, sliders), a render check that walks every step at phone and desktop widths and flags overlaps, moving controls and errors, and a recorder that turns the explainer into MP4 and GIF. Use it whenever you build any animated diagram, step-by-step lesson, interactive explainer or explainer GIF, together with the form skill chosen by explainer-director.
- stepper-lesson: Build a Khan Academy style step-by-step animated lesson that teaches one idea per step, with a Next button, short captions, and one or two steps the reader plays (tap, toggle, mini-game). Use when someone wants to teach how something works or why something changed to a newcomer - a concept, a mechanism, a rule, a workflow, a security trick, a statistics idea - as an interactive explainer, a "lesson", a walkthrough, or a GIF for social media. Pairs with explainer-director (plan) and explainer-kit (engine and checks).
- system-replay: Build an animated replay of how a system runs - AI agents and subagents, an agent harness with tools and guardrails, a data pipeline, an API request path, a queue, a protocol - from recorded logs or traces (timestamps, spans, events) or from a designed demonstration run. Shows actors, calls travelling between them, waiting and blocking, hidden state like a context window or budget, and a live event log, on a timeline the reader can pause and scrub. Use when someone wants to visualize an agent trace, show who waited for whom, compare coordination patterns, explain how a harness or architecture works in motion, or turn run logs into an animation or GIF.
- explorable-model: Build an explorable explanation - a live model the reader steers with one or two sliders or toggles while linked views show the outcome change - for questions where the answer depends on a choice or an assumption: a price, a threshold, a rate, a budget, an error cost, a break-even point, a tradeoff between options. Use when someone wants an interactive "what if" calculator that teaches, a break-even or crossover explainer, a sensitivity visual, or asks to let readers "play with the numbers", "see when it pays off" or "find where the answer flips", from data or a cost model.
- unit-story: Build an animated unit visualization where every item is its own mark - each test question, request, invoice, user or sample is a dot - and the dots move between arrangements (one pile, split by model, coloured by outcome, sorted) to tell a story about counts, composition and uncertainty. Includes showing small-sample uncertainty honestly with intervals and hypothetical outcome animations. Use when someone wants to visualize evaluation or benchmark results per item, compare models on a test set, show how many of N failed, explain why "0 out of N" or a small sample does not prove much, or make results tangible for a LinkedIn post, slide or README.
- predict-reveal: Build a "guess first, then see the data" interactive - the reader draws a line, drags a bar, places a dot or picks an option to commit to a prediction, then the real data animates in against their guess with the gap explained, followed by how models or methods did. Use when a result is surprising or counterintuitive, when you want readers to remember a number, for forecasting and time series explainers ("draw what you think happens next"), quiz-like engagement for social posts, or when someone asks for something more engaging than a chart about a single key finding.
