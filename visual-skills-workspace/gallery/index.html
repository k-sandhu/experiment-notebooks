Explainer Skills Lab

Seven agent skills for turning a finding, a process or a dataset into an animated, interactive explainer that a newcomer can follow on a phone. They grew out of the chalkboard animations of an agentic finance harness and a lesson on deep work versus parallel agents.

To test them, the same model built five explainers from this repository's experiment data with the skills and without them, and a separate model compared each pair blind. After round 1, the reviews' complaints went into the skills and the five were rebuilt (round 2) against the same versions made without the skills. Round 2 and the no-skills versions are live below, side by side, with the round-1 version one click away. Eight more notebooks built with the skills follow.

The skills

  • explainer-directorFinds the one message, picks the form, writes a storyboard with one idea per step.
  • explainer-kitEngine, four styles, scaffold, render check at phone and desktop widths, GIF/MP4 recorder.
  • stepper-lessonKhan Academy style lesson: Next, short captions, one or two steps the reader plays.
  • system-replayRecorded runs replayed on a timeline you can pause and scrub, or a designed walkthrough.
  • explorable-modelA live model the reader steers with a slider; the break-even point is marked.
  • unit-storyEvery item is a dot; dots regroup by outcome; small samples show their uncertainty.
  • predict-revealThe reader commits a guess, then the real data animates in against it.

Why a path check can be fooled

I want a short, interactive Khan Academy style lesson for non-engineers that explains why a file-permission gate that only checks how a path starts can be tricked, and what the safer gate designs do differently. Use the results from our notebook 10 (the data and key facts are in the input files). It should work well on a phone, and I'd like to be able to turn it into a GIF for LinkedIn later.

Reviewer: Both are accurate against facts.md (5 of 14 for the prefix gate, 0 of 14 / 8 of 8 for scoped and approval bound, read only 4 of 8, the ceiling-by-design and untested-hardlink caveats). B is the better Khan-style interactive lesson: its step-2 quiz and the five-trick deck in step 3 take a guess, say 'Right' or 'Not quite' and explain, then animate a walk of the path through the map; step 4 lets the reader pick or type any path and see the starts-with gate and the safer gate decide side by side with reasons (editing a path to tmp/../output/x.txt correctly flips the verdicts); step 5's scoreboard squares open the real request ID and decision. B's step 6 also explains why 5 of 14 is a list of tricks rather than a failure rate, which A only implies.

Econ 21 · an interactive for finance managers

When checking a cheap model pays off

Build an interactive explainer for finance managers: when does it pay to let a cheap AI model handle invoice payments and only send the doubtful ones to the expensive model? Let them change the price of the expensive model and see which checking strategy is cheapest. Use the 72 test invoices from our econ notebook 21 (input files).

Reviewer: Both pages are accurate against facts.md: A's step 4 and 5 bills (4.05c vs 5c vs 6.62c, break-evens 0.11c and 13.3c) and B's monthly figures ($405 vs $500 vs $662 at $0.05, $4,030/$4,287 at $0.50, break-evens $0.0011 and $0.133) all recompute correctly. B fits finance managers better. Its calculator turns the GPT price into a monthly bill at a volume the reader types in. It splits each bar into the measured cheap-model-and-check cost and the expensive calls at your price. It writes a plain verdict sentence ('ask-twice is cheapest: $405 a month, $95 less...'), adds price presets including both break-evens, and ends with a clear 'Before you rely on this' list. A is a tidy six-step lesson with a good 14-of-72 dot sequence, and it shows the Wilson 12-30% interval that B leaves out. But its key slider step packs a log-scale share-of-bill chart and cent prices into very small type, which is harder for this audience to read. B's step 5 layout overflows at phone width, a real defect, but it does not outweigh B's clearer price-to-decision payoff.

Notebook 05 · a LinkedIn visual

Zero out of 29 is not proof

I need a visual for a LinkedIn post about notebook 05: 11 models each got 29 requests that would break the database rules. Most broke zero rules, but I want people to understand that zero out of 29 doesn't prove a model is safe. Make it engaging and honest about the uncertainty. Data is in the input files.

Reviewer: Both pages get the main finding across with correct numbers: 9 of 11 at 0 of 29, Wilson 0-12% (about 1 in 8), 23% chance of a clean run at a 1-in-20 rate, and 73/381 clean tries. B is the more engaging piece. Its step 4 asks the reader to guess before the 20 simulated tests run, and 'Run 20 more tests' builds up a running tally (20 of 80 clean). Its step 5 per-model interval chart with '9 models, same score, same range' makes the point that the test cannot rank models. A won because it is more complete and more honest for a public post. Step 3 shows three real trap requests, and step 4's grid of 319 replies by category adds the 23 ordinary questions so the clean scores are not read as blanket refusal. The takeaway names the other limits: one reply per request, rules spelled out in the prompt, no breaks on the 13 write/file requests (ceiling), and the 'refused' label that cannot be trusted. B ends with only the tries-needed chart, and in steps 4 and 6 it reuses its 'Refused, or did only the allowed part' colour for simulated runs and tries bars. A's always-visible luck slider is accurate (1% gives 75%, 10% gives 4.7%) but sits outside the card and does not change the scenes.

More experiments

Eight more notebooks, each built once with the revised skills from the notebook's README and data, with no human edits. Tap a card to open it full size.

What two rounds of reviews taught

Across both rounds the version with the skills won 5 of the 10 blind comparisons. The skills made layout reliable: 54 of 55 automatic checks passed in each round with them, against 48 of 55 without, and only the skills versions' replays fit their whole timeline on a phone. The versions made without the skills won by going deeper for their audience: a monthly bill at the reader's own volume, a quiz that marks the reader's answer, a path the reader types in, a list of what the test could not show.

Round 1 changes, tested in round 2: the social-feed case became a self-playing card, the forecast now ends with how often the models' ranges held across all forecasts, and the guardrail lesson asks before each reveal. Changes from round 2, now in the skills but not yet re-tested:

  • A question step waits for the reader and marks the answer right or wrong; the idle demo no longer answers for them (the guardrail case was lost on this).
  • Give an explorable's outcome in the reader's units, such as dollars per month at a volume they type in (the invoice case was lost on this).
  • End with the test's limits in plain words: items, runs per item, ceilings, what the setup made easy (the SQL case was lost on this, twice).
  • Labels at least 13 units (about 11 px on a phone); the render check now warns under 10.5 px. The legend shows only the colours a step uses. No internal run names on screen.

How far to trust this

Each case was built once per condition in each round, and round 2 reused the same no-skills versions, so the five-versus-five result could easily swing either way with more runs. The reviewer is also a model. Read the comparison as a map of what the skills change, not a measurement of how much.

The skill files contain no data or wording from the five test notebooks, so the runs with skills could not copy an answer. The pages here are copies of each run's output, unedited.
