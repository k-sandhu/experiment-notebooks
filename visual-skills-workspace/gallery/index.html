Explainer Skills Lab

Seven agent skills for turning a finding, a process or a dataset into an animated, interactive explainer that a newcomer can follow on a phone. They grew out of the chalkboard animations of an agentic finance harness and a lesson on deep work versus parallel agents.

To test them, the same model built five explainers from this repository's experiment data with the skills and without them, and a separate model compared each pair blind. After each round, the reviews' complaints went into the skills and the five were rebuilt, always against the same versions made without the skills. Round 3 and the no-skills versions are live below, side by side, with the round-1 and round-2 versions one click away. Eight more notebooks built with the skills follow.

The skills

  • explainer-directorFinds the one message, picks the form, writes a storyboard with one idea per step.
  • explainer-kitEngine, four styles, scaffold, render check at phone and desktop widths, GIF/MP4 recorder.
  • stepper-lessonKhan Academy style lesson: Next, short captions, one or two steps the reader plays.
  • system-replayRecorded runs replayed on a timeline you can pause and scrub, or a designed walkthrough.
  • explorable-modelA live model the reader steers with a slider; the break-even point is marked.
  • unit-storyEvery item is a dot; dots regroup by outcome; small samples show their uncertainty.
  • predict-revealThe reader commits a guess, then the real data animates in against it.

Notebook 10 · a lesson for non-engineers

Why a path check can be fooled

I want a short, interactive Khan Academy style lesson for non-engineers that explains why a file-permission gate that only checks how a path starts can be tricked, and what the safer gate designs do differently. Use the results from our notebook 10 (the data and key facts are in the input files). It should work well on a phone, and I'd like to be able to turn it into a GIF for LinkedIn later.

Reviewer: Both are accurate (5 of 14 for the prefix gate, 0 of 14 for the scoped gates, read only 4 of 8, scoped and approval bound indistinguishable, ceiling-by-design caveat), and A's fixed dark chalkboard stage with an animated folder map is the more GIF-ready of the two. B teaches better for a non-engineer. In its step 3, each of the five tricks is its own guess with 'Right' or 'Not quite' feedback, a plain-language reason and a step-by-step walk of the path. Trick 4 there explains why order matters (enter the link, then go up, so you land in /srv/private.txt and not where text tidying says). A packs four tricks into one auto-cycling step 4 with a single sentence, never explains the link-then-parent order, and its step 3 guess feedback does not tell a wrong guesser they were wrong. B's gate tester shows the reasoning of each gate ('Is that inside /workspace/acme/tmp/? No'). Its scoreboard lets you tap any of the 110 squares to see the actual request, and its last screen frames 5 of 14 as 'a list of tricks it misses, not a failure rate'. That makes B the more Khan-style lesson, although its long scrolling views make it harder to turn into a GIF.

More experiments

Eight more notebooks, each built once with the revised skills from the notebook's README and data, with no human edits. Tap a card to open it full size.

What three rounds of reviews taught

Across three rounds the version with the skills won 8 of the 15 blind comparisons (3, 2, then 3 of 5). Its summed review score was higher in every round: 110 to 105, 109 to 103, then 112 to 100 out of 125. The skills made layout reliable: 54 of 55 automatic checks passed in each round with them, against 48 of 55 without. The replay and the forecast won all three rounds; the guardrail lesson and the SQL card lost all three.

The round-2 changes, tested in round 3:

  • Outcome in the reader's units. Worked: the invoice explorable now gives a monthly bill at a volume the reader picks, and it won back the case (24 to 19).
  • Text size, legends, no internal names. Worked: no tiny-text failures with the skills in any case, and the reviewers no longer flagged run names.
  • End with the test's limits. Followed, but the SQL case still lost by one point. The reviewer preferred the other version's fixed-size card for a LinkedIn post and its note that the models still answered ordinary questions, which shows the clean scores were not refusals.
  • Question steps mark the reader's answer. Only half followed: the guardrail lesson now waits for the guess, but its feedback reads the same whatever the reader picks, and it packs four tricks into one auto-playing step where the other version gives each trick its own guess.

How far to trust this

Each case was built once per condition in each round, and all three rounds reused the same no-skills versions, so the 8-of-15 result could easily swing either way with more runs. The reviewer is also a model, and it scored the same no-skills page differently from round to round (the invoice page got 21, 21, then 19), so differences of one or two points are noise. Read the comparison as a map of what the skills change, not a measurement of how much.

The skill files contain no data or wording from the five test notebooks, so the runs with skills could not copy an answer. The pages here are copies of each run's output, unedited.
