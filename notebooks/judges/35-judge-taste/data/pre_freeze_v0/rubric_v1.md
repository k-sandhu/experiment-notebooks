Rubric for judging edits to explanatory and technical prose

Judge which version a careful, plain-spoken editor would accept. The editor writes for an intelligent non-specialist who needs to know exactly what was done, what it shows and what it does not show. Many accepted versions are unchanged or barely changed, so restraint is a virtue. Do not reward rewriting for its own sake. Penalise edits that disturb text that was already clear, accurate and well placed.

Priorities, in rough order:

1. Accurate scope of claims. The strongest repeated change is stating precisely what the evidence covers, often in an "X, not Y" form: "this compares routes, not the models' abilities", "shows what was written, not why", "measured whether quotations were copied, not whether it helps a reviewer". Prefer the version that names the limit plainly and ties it to the reason. Added caveats that prevent overreading are welcome. Caveats that are vague, hedged everywhere, or duplicated are not.

2. Concrete, everyday wording over abstraction. Prefer named things to category words, such as "units billed, received and returned" over "quantities". Prefer people to roles ("a journalist" over "someone writing a news report"), short common words ("cheaper", "made up", "reading") and exact figures. Keep exact numbers, quoted source text, code identifiers and commands. Plainness never means losing precision.

3. Cut what the reader does not need. Remove sentences that restate the previous one, that tell the reader what a calculation "establishes", or that moralise after an example has made its point. Remove process trivia that does not affect interpretation, such as parser mechanics or file-preservation notes. Compress future-work and limitation items to three parts: what to do, what to measure, and why the current evidence falls short.

4. Orient the reader. Add a short lead-in before lists, quoted blocks, tables and code ("There are four kinds of answer."). Put the question first, then the evidence, then the answer. Before defining a tool or term, motivate why it is needed. On first use, define a term in everyday language, bold it once, and rule out a misleading sense if one exists. Flag invented examples as invented at the start. Walking one item through a process from start to finish beats an abstract description.

5. Plain structure. Turn bold label lead-ins in bullets and paragraphs into ordinary full sentences. Recommendations should be direct and actionable, often imperative. Explain the mechanism behind a surprising figure, such as why a total can be negative, rather than only asserting it.

6. Placement. The editor sometimes moves a sentence rather than deleting it, putting a summary after the material it summarises or a caveat before the results it qualifies. When a pair differs only in where a sentence sits, prefer the version where each statement follows what it depends on.

Tradeoffs:
- Brevity against explanation. Shorter is better when the removed text is redundant, procedural or self-congratulatory. Longer is better when the addition defines a term, motivates a step, scopes a claim or introduces a block. Judge what the words do, not the word count.
- Simplicity against precision. Never accept a simplification that drops a qualifier, number or condition the claim depends on.
- Tone. Prefer calm, factual statements. Penalise rhetorical framing and emphatic labels, and avoid stronger claims than the evidence supports. Tightening a vague claim to an exact one ("any work" rather than "much work") is fine when it is accurate.

What not to infer:
- Do not assume the editor dislikes technical content. Commands, file names, identifiers and arithmetic stay when they serve the reader.
- Do not assume a preference for shorter text in every case, or for removing caveats or quotations.
- Do not treat spelling variety (British or American) as a criterion. Only inconsistency within a piece matters.
- Do not reward changing quoted prompts or source text. Verbatim quotations should stay verbatim.
- Do not judge subject matter, conclusions or formatting conventions such as tables of contents or link style, unless they affect clarity.
- If both versions are equally clear and accurate, prefer the one closer to the original.
