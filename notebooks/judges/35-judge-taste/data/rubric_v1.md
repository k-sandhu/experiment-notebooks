What this editor values

The editor writes for an intelligent outsider. Every change aims to let a non-specialist follow the reasoning on first read, without losing exact figures or honesty about limits. Prefer the version a newcomer could understand and retell correctly.

Priorities, roughly in order

1. Give the reader the context they need before the detail.
The editor often adds a framing sentence or paragraph rather than rewriting what follows. Examples: a one-line lead-in before a list ("There are four kinds of answer."), a sentence saying what a block of commands does, a short explanation of what a tool or actor is before it appears, or the question an analysis answers stated up front. Findings get a headline sentence before the supporting bullets. When an enumeration promises cases, all of them should be present. Reward versions that orient the reader. Penalize versions that drop the reader into specifics, jargon or an incomplete set.

2. Be concrete, not abstract.
The editor consistently replaces general descriptions with the actual thing: quoted instructions instead of paraphrased constraints, the real list of fields instead of a category summary, actual rules as bullets, a worked walk-through of one case, and a tangible scale ("a small fraction of a cent per invoice") instead of "costs less". Open with the concrete question being answered, not with an asserted result.

3. Use plain words over insider or academic vocabulary.
Typical swaps are "made up" or "invented" for "illustrative" or "synthetic", "test" for "held-out", "answer key" for "gold", "folder" for "directory", "double counting" for "duplicate counting", "document type" for "genre", and "need" or "ask" for "require" or "request". Technical terms are kept only when the reader needs them, and then they are defined in place, sometimes in bold at first use, with a note on what the term does not mean. Code, identifiers, commands and exact numbers are kept intact.

4. Cut what does not earn its place.
Remove closing sentences that restate, justify or editorialize ("These calculations establish…", "These words help us keep track…"). Remove redundant restatements of a point already made, procedural trivia the reader cannot use, and sentences that tell readers what to think rather than showing it. Merge choppy runs of short sentences into fewer, connected ones. Do not penalize a version for losing a paragraph when what remains is complete and coherent. The editor removes material freely.

5. Keep the sharp interpretive line.
Cutting is not flattening. The editor keeps or adds one crisp sentence that says why something matters: "Both numbers are true", "a mistake made twice looks like agreement", "The arithmetic is usually the easy part." A version with a clear takeaway beats a merely neutral one.

6. Be honest and specific about limits and mechanisms.
Weaknesses are stated plainly, with their cause ("checks patterns in the files rather than running code, so…") and what was done about them ("every affected run was collected again"). Clear statements of what a process does not do ("makes no model calls") are welcome. Claims should be exact rather than loosely hedged: "100 or more", "without removing any work".

7. Use structure that fits the content.
Limitations, recommendations and parallel rules tend to become bullets written as full sentences. Bold run-in labels at the start of bullets are removed in favour of a natural opening sentence. Explanatory narrative stays in prose. Made-up examples are labelled plainly as made up.

Tradeoffs
- Brevity versus context: the editor both cuts and adds. Judge whether each sentence helps a newcomer understand, not whether the text got shorter or longer.
- Plainness versus precision: simplify the wording, never the numbers, identifiers or logical distinctions.
- Unchanged text is often accepted as is. A version that leaves good prose alone and adds only the missing frame should beat a gratuitous rewrite.

What not to infer
- Do not reward length or brevity for its own sake.
- Do not treat spelling variety (British or American) as a preference. Both appear.
- Do not assume bullets are always better. They are favoured for parallel items and caveats, not for explanation.
- Do not penalize technical content, code formatting or precise figures. Penalize only undefined jargon and abstraction where something concrete was available.
- Do not judge removed material as lost information if it was redundant, meta or unusable by the reader.
- Do not prefer warmer or more casual tone in itself. The target is clear, direct and plain, not chatty.
